# Historical transmission routes: evidence policy

Routes on Plate IV are **not migration tracks, political borders, or claims about cultural ownership**. They are deliberately sparse visual summaries of a documented writing-system adoption, adaptation, or transmission corridor between two historical origin records already in the atlas.

Every route has one of three statuses. **Attested transmission** is used where the relationship and corridor are well documented in the historical record. **Probable transmission** is used where the atlas already records a likely descent but specialists dispute a direct mechanism or a precise corridor. **Cultural adaptation** is used where a recipient writing practice was developed through an external script tradition, without pretending that the line is a literal journey made by a single alphabet.

The map will begin with a small, readable register of twelve high-consequence routes rather than draw every genealogical edge. A route joins the historical points used by the origin map, but is visibly curved and labelled in its annotation; it is a schematic historical corridor, not a measured road. Route dates identify the earliest relevant adoption or development period, not a duration of travel.

| Route | Status | Historical claim | Primary framing source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Phoenician → Greek | Attested transmission | Greek adoption and adaptation in early Archaic Greece, probably through Euboean contact. | [History of the Greek alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Greek_alphabet) |
| Greek → Old Italic → Latin | Attested transmission | Western/Euboean Greek moved through Italy; Etruscan adaptation underlies Latin. | [History of the Greek alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Greek_alphabet) |
| Phoenician → Aramaic | Attested transmission | Alphabetic writing spread in the Near East through Phoenician and Aramaic lingua-franca use. | [Cambridge, 2008](https://doi.org/10.1177/0392192108090738) |
| Aramaic → Nabataean → Arabic | Attested transmission | A sequence already represented by the atlas’s historical descent edges. | [Arabic script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_script) |
| Aramaic → Brahmi | Probable transmission | A proposed ancestry, not a settled direct journey; plotted only as a qualified long-range link. | [Brahmi script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahmi_script) |
| Brahmi → Tibetan | Attested transmission | Tibetan creation draws on Indian models through a documented Indic corridor. | [Tibetan script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tibetan_script) |
| Pallava → Khmer → Thai/Lao | Cultural adaptation | South Indian-derived forms developed through mainland Southeast Asian textual networks. | [Pallava script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pallava_script) |
| Pallava → Kawi → Javanese/Balinese | Cultural adaptation | Indic-derived writing developed through insular Southeast Asian traditions. | [Pallava script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pallava_script) |
| Chinese characters → Korean / Japanese / Vietnamese traditions | Attested transmission | Chinese-character literacy spread across the East Asian textual sphere. | [Cambridge Handbook chapter](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-handbook-of-language-standardization/language-modernization-in-the-chinese-character-cultural-sphere/C78FF061927EEED8A7FA001A4E6710AC) |
| Sogdian → Old Uyghur → Mongolian | Attested transmission | The Uyghur adaptation of Sogdian and Mongolian adoption through Uyghur scribal practice. | [Old Uyghur script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Uyghur_script) |
| Greek → Coptic | Attested transmission | Coptic was created in Egypt from Greek forms supplemented with Demotic signs. | [Coptic alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coptic_alphabet) |
| Greek → Cyrillic | Attested transmission | Early Cyrillic draws directly on Greek writing in the Slavic Christian literary context. | [Cyrillic script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyrillic_script) |

The Cambridge overview describes the preservation of linear alphabetic scripts in Aramaic and Phoenician and traces Phoenician influence through Greek, Etruscan, and Latin. It supports the map’s restrained Mediterranean and Near Eastern corridors rather than an indiscriminate web of all possible similarities. The Asian routes use the same evidentiary caution: they mark adoption and adaptation, not a universal or monocausal account of cultural change.
