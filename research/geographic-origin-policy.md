# Geographic origin plate: evidence policy

The map records **where a script was historically developed or first securely attested**, not the full modern footprint of the languages it writes. Script use can be transregional; the point is an interpretive historical origin, not a claim of ethnic ownership or contemporary national entitlement.

Coordinates therefore use three explicit precision levels. **Site** refers to a known archaeological or documentary locus (for example Uruk, Abydos, Anyang, or Ugarit). **Region** denotes a culturally and historically meaningful area when the evidence does not sustain a single city (for example Gandhara or the Deccan). **Broad** identifies an approximate cultural zone for scripts whose beginnings are debated, geographically diffuse, or only partially understood. The detail leaf will surface this language rather than suggesting false precision.

The atlas’s existing per-script source URL remains the source trail for each record. The map inherits its global historical framing from the Oriental Institute’s account of four independent inventions, the Penn Museum’s synthesis of the alphabet’s Levantine origin and later spread, and *The World’s Writing Systems* (Daniels and Bright, 1996). Those sources support the map’s distinction between origin and later distribution; individual point placements will be cross-checked against each script’s existing regional field and source record.

## Design decision

The geographic view will be **Plate IV — Places of Invention**. It will use a muted, built-in world outline rather than a contemporary road or political base map. Map markers will use the atlas’s branch pigments; their positions will be gently displaced only when several historically distinct origins would otherwise overlap. A small register will state the number of mapped records and those kept intentionally off-map because no responsible origin point can be named.
